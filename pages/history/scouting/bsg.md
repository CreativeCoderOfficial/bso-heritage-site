## Origins

Early in the winter of 1946, a small conference took place at IHQ [Imperial Scout Headquarters] as it was then known, to discuss the best way to provide Scouting for the boys of the married families who were going out to the British Army of the Rhine [BAOR] under 'Operation Union'. Mr John Trevalyan, Director of the British Families Education Service for the BAOR and a member of the IHQ Advisory panel, was present and outlined the expected education scheme. Mr Trevalyan accepted the invitation to become Scout Commissioner for the Zone [Scout County], with the services of a full-time Field Commissioner.

Up to March 1947 there had been a few scattered Service Rover Crews in the Zone, who were doing excellent work and had started to run Cub Packs and Scout Troops. In March 1947 a Zonal Office was set up at Herford, and organisation commenced. A Scout Commissioner was found for each of the five Regions [Districts] - Berlin, Schleswig-Holstein, Hamburg, Land Niedersachsen and Land North Rhine Westphalia.

There were some 4,500 children attending British Schools in the Zone, of whom about 1,500 were boys of Cub or Scout age. The difficulties were many; in the first place the British Zone covered an area of 37,721 square miles, and the children were scattered over the whole Zone. One school could draw its children from an area of 80 square miles; buses brought them to school and took them home. Cubbing or Scouting either had to take place before the children returned home or the buses had to collect them together again in the evening. The Scouters were mainly young Rovers in the services, and it was difficult for them to fit in meeting times with their work patterns. Another problem was that Scouters were frequently posted or demobbed; the boys too moved with their parents.

There were now about 36 Groups in the Zone with about 500 Cubs, 6 Scouts and 400 Rovers. Many of the Scouts had gone to boarding school at Wilhelmshaven, in northern Germany where up to then, the Headmaster had not allowed a Scout Troop to be formed.

Another boarding school was to be opened at Easter at Plon, where there were excellent prospects of Scouting. Several Cub and Scout Preliminary Wood Badge Training Courses had already been conducted in various parts of the Zone and over 100 men and women had attended. One Cub and one Scout Wood Badge Course had been held. Running one of these courses could mean a journey of over 400 miles for the Training Team and equipment had to be collected from various sources over a wide area. Transport and the supply of rations also presented a problem. The Camp Chief from Gilwell visited the Wood Badge Courses.

Several Cub and Scout camps had been held, and a contingent of Scouts and Scouters attended the 6th World Jamboree at Moisson in France. A Zonal Rover Moot was held in autumn 1947, coinciding with a week's visit of the General Secretary from IHQ. 50 Rovers spent Christmas in the Hartz mountains, and a party of Senior Scouts spent a week at Winterberg in early January.

The finances of the Zone were fairly strong as the Army and the RAF each subscribed £200, which has been shared with the Girl Guides. The Zonal Office had set up an equipment department for books and badges etc. A monthly magazine the 'Fleur de Lys' was published and circulated over the whole Zone.

At Easter 1948 there was to be a rally at Hamburg, which the Chief Scout Lord Rowallan, promised to attend. Guests had been invited from the American and French Zones, D.P. [German] Scouts including Latvian, Lithuanian, Estonian, Polish, Ukrainian [these five were mainly displaced Scouts due to the war]; also Belgian and Norwegian Rovers from the British Zone. It is expected that there will be 600 present.
